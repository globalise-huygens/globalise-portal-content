# Project & ambitions

<!-- Source: Matthias van Rossum and Manjusha Kuruppath, "Mission, Research Themes and Guiding Questions", version 1.0, 18 October 2024, https://docs.globalise.huygens.knaw.nl/mission/mission_researchthemes/ (CC BY-SA 4.0). This page is a condensed adaptation. -->

The archives of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) are among the richest sources for the early modern history of Asia, Africa and the Indian Ocean world. The Globalise project was set up to make these archives available in new ways, and to change what kind of history can be written from them. This page describes the project's three main ambitions and the research themes that guided its work.

## From searchability to researchability

Most digitised archives let users search for words in transcribed text. That is useful, but it has clear limits for the VOC archives. Names and places were spelled in many different ways, the same polity might appear as Banten, Bantam or Bantamm, and a keyword search cannot tell a user who a person was, when a ruler reigned or what a now-obscure commodity actually was.

The project therefore combined computational methods, such as text recognition and language models, with *historical contextualisation*: curated, checked historical knowledge brought together in reference datasets and thesauri on polities, rulers, people, places, commodities, and weights and measures. Linking the archive to this knowledge makes it possible to ask questions like "Which polities were in conflict with the VOC between 1640 and 1690?" and receive results with context, not just a list of pages that contain a word.

These results describe what, where and when. Answering questions about how and why remains the work of the researcher, who still has to read, interpret and contextualise the sources.

## Open and inclusive access

The project aimed to keep technical barriers low, so that the infrastructure could be used by researchers, students and interested members of the public around the world, not only by specialists in Dutch history or digital methods. Data, models and software were published openly wherever possible.

The project's name reflects this ambition. Originally an acronym for the technical approach planned at the start of the project (General Letters Ontology-Based Accessibility InfraStructurE), it is also a reminder that globalisation has always been unequal. Both the "early globalisation" that accelerated with European colonial expansion and the digital globalisation of today have produced connection alongside dispossession and exploitation. The project sought to work against this by building openly, by reaching out to scholars worldwide and by examining the colonial biases that have shaped which knowledge was produced, and which knowledge has been made available in digital form.

## A different history of the VOC

The VOC has long been remembered in the Netherlands mainly as a merchant company focused on trade and shipping. This picture leaves out much of what the company did: it waged war and conducted diplomacy, governed territories and subject populations, organised plantation agriculture and mining, and was deeply involved in slavery and the slave trade. It was a colonial power as much as a trading company.

The Globalise project aimed to support research that takes this fuller picture seriously. Above all, it aimed to move the VOC away from the centre of attention and to make it easier to study the polities and societies of the Indian Ocean world as historical subjects in their own right: how they interacted with the VOC and with each other, and how they were changed by those interactions. To support this, the project invested in reference data on non-European polities, rulers, people, commodities and communities.

## Guiding questions and research themes

Three questions guided the design of the infrastructure:

* Why and how did interactions between European colonial actors and local societies develop according to particular historical patterns?
* How did expanding European influence affect political, social, economic, environmental and cultural developments in the Indian Ocean, the Indonesian archipelago and the East Asian seas?
* How can the VOC archives be used to their fullest potential in writing histories of local, non-Western societies?

From these questions, the project defined a set of thematic domains. They determined which reference data, vocabularies and event types were developed first.

| Domain | Examples of research areas |
| ------ | -------------------------- |
| Political | Colonial expansion; non-European states; diplomacy and treaties; war, genocide and depopulation |
| Economic | Production of commodities such as spices, sugar, coffee and indigo; trade and merchant networks; finance and taxation |
| Social | Slavery and the slave trade; labour; gender |
| Cultural | Religion; intercultural interaction and representation; circulation of knowledge |
| Environmental | Weather and natural disasters; animals; famine |

Political and economic history may look like conventional choices. They reflect what appears most often in the archive. But within these domains, the project deliberately drew attention to lesser-known Asian polities and to the more invasive aspects of colonialism, rather than only to Dutch trade and shipping.

Any such selection is subjective. The themes reflect the content of the source material, the needs of a broad range of researchers, and the potential to open up new perspectives, particularly on non-European societies and on histories of colonisation and encounter.

## Building responsibly

Making an archive easier to search also influences what people search for, what they find and which histories get written. Given the colonial bias of the VOC archives, this brings responsibilities. The project's curated data reflects the knowledge and perspectives of the people who made it, and much of the annotation was done automatically and could only be partly checked by hand. The project therefore aimed to be transparent about its methods, its choices and its limitations. These issues are discussed in the section on [Ethics & colonial context](/pages/research-responsibly/ethics/index.md).
