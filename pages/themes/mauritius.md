## Introduction to Mauritius 

To the east of the African continent sits the island nation of Mauritius. Dutch sailors first encountered the island in 1598 and named it Mauritius, after the Prince Maurice of Nassau. In the years that followed, it was visited by ships en-route to Asia. In 1637, it was decided that a formal settlement should be established on the island. From 1638 until 1658, the island was a site for various VOC activities including the building of Fort Hendrik, the extraction of resources like ebony, and agricultural activities such as the cultivation of sugar. These endeavours were met with various obstacles, and gradually the population of the island decreased until 1658, when it was essentially abandoned. A possible explanation for the declining interest during this time is the success of the Cape Colony which had been founded in 1652\. A second attempt at colonisation occurred in 1664, however adverse conditions led to the formal abandonment of the colony by 1710\. 

The Dutch period in Mauritius has remained relatively understudied in relation to French and British occupation. The early years of Dutch settlement resulted in several crucial events that have had lasting effects on the island in the present. VOC archives offer the opportunity to investigate important events such as the slave uprising in 1695, the ecological impact of deforestation and effects on the local flora and fauna of the island. Additionally, VOC archives reveal the connections between Mauritius, Asia and continental Africa, which can provide crucial insight into the early activities of what would become a VOC empire. 

## The GLOBALISE portal  

The GLOBALISE portal offers wider access to a rich repository of VOC archives which aid in understanding the early modern period, in which archival sources are often relatively scarce. Within the GLOBALISE database, users can access a variety of sources concerning VOC activities in Mauritius. 

### Using GLOBALISE and uncovering Mauritius 

There are several key figures which can help contextualise VOC involvement with and on the island. Particularly relevant are the former ‘commanders’ such as Corneliasz Simonsz Gooyer and Adriaan Van der Stel. Archives also reveal the use of Mauritius in naming VOC ships; we see reference to names such as *Eiland Mauritius* and *Mauritius.* This may complicate research intended to focus exclusively on Mauritius. Users interested in slavery in the VOC world can find connections between Madagascar, Mauritius, Batavia and the Cape of Good Hope, which are referenced in the daily registers kept by the Commanders at these settlements. Within the OBP and most VOC archives, the content is mostly institutional and administrative, while alternate perspectives appear in indirect mentions. These perspectives may appear most apparent in sites of ‘maroonage’, which were places that the enslaved population created outside of European occupation, and material that may best represent these communities can be found in material remains. The Le Morne Cultural Landscape on the island of Mauritius offers a powerful visual representation of resistance to slavery.

## Connecting Mauritius to the world

The island of Mauritius became a crucial settlement in the Indian Ocean World and was the site of various colonial projects which have shaped the contemporary world. Prior to the arrival of VOC ships, Mauritius was visited by Arab seafarers. After its initial formal settlement in the 17th century, it grew to become one of the most important sources of sugar production by the 18th century.  Mauritius is also considered the site of the beginning of the indentured labour diaspora that proliferated in the 19th century. These periods provide an entry point into the rich connections between the East African coast, the Atlantic and Caribbean regions through themes in history. These aid in understanding connections across the Indian Ocean Basin, but also trans-oceanic perspectives. 

## Related literature 

There are several key authors that can provide useful historical context, although most literature is concerned with the French period, these texts address the foundations of the island and its consolidation as a node in the trade activities of the Indian Ocean. Some of the key works include:

* {*Mauritius on the Spice Route, 1598-1810* by Denis Piat (2010)}  
* *Bitter Sugar: Sugar and Slavery in 19th century Mauritius* by Vijaya Teelock (1998)  
* *History of the Indian Ocean* by Auguste Toussaint (1966)  
* *A New Comprehensive History of Mauritius* by Sydney Selvon (2019)
