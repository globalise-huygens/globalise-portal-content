# Colonial context

<!-- Sources: pages/resources/voc-archives-guide.md (Manjusha Kuruppath); "Mission, Research Themes and Guiding Questions" v1.0 (Matthias van Rossum and Manjusha Kuruppath, pages/about/archive/mission-research-themes.md); Globalise Ethics Policy v2.0, including the ethics report on the Places Dataset (Ruben Land and Manjusha Kuruppath), pages/ethics/ethics-policy.md. -->

The archives of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) are colonial archives. To use them well, it helps to understand what kind of organisation the VOC was, how and why it produced its records, and how that shaped what the records say, and do not say, about the people and societies the company encountered.

## The VOC as a colonial power

The VOC was founded in the Dutch Republic in 1602 to trade with Asia and to carry the Republic's war with the Habsburg empire to a new theatre. Until it was dissolved in 1799, it built a network of fortifications, trading posts and colonies across Asia and Africa.

The VOC is still often remembered mainly as a merchant company concerned with trade and shipping. That picture is incomplete. The company waged wars and conducted diplomacy, governed territories and their populations, and organised the production of commodities. It was a colonial power in Taiwan, Java, the Moluccas, Sri Lanka and parts of Malabar in southern India. It established a settler colony at the Cape of Good Hope, where it subdued and dispossessed local populations, and it controlled Mauritius. In other places, such as Iran, most of India and Japan, it operated as a trader.

Many long-standing economic, social, political and cultural conflicts in these regions have their origins in this period of emerging colonialism. This is one reason why the VOC archives remain important today, and why they should be read with this history in mind.

## A colonial archive

The VOC produced an enormous written record. Its officials documented day-to-day events, government resolutions, trade, diplomacy and justice, and copied this correspondence from settlement to settlement and on to the company's directors in the Dutch Republic. This "paper empire" was essential for running a trading and colonial enterprise across a vast maritime region.

The *Overgekomen Brieven en Papieren* (OBP), the series that Globalise works with, is a product of this system. It contains the letters and papers sent from the company's headquarters in Batavia (present-day Jakarta) and from other settlements to the Dutch Republic. The [guide to the VOC archives](/pages/resources/voc-archives-guide.md) explains how the company's administration and correspondence were organised.

Because of how they came into being, the documents record the past as the company and its personnel encountered, interpreted and wrote about it. They reflect the company's interests and the political, religious and racial prejudices of the people who wrote them. They are not a neutral record, and their contents should not be taken as the truth.

## Other voices in the archive

The archive does contain the voices of local individuals and communities: in court testimonies, in letters from rulers and merchants, and in diplomatic agreements. This makes it an important source for local histories, including for regions where few other written sources survive.

But these voices reach us through the company. Statements and letters were often translated from local languages into Dutch, and they were preserved because they mattered to the colonial authorities, not because they represented the people concerned. Reading them requires the same critical attention as any other part of the archive.

Globalise aims to support research that moves the VOC away from the centre of attention, and treats the polities and societies of the Indian Ocean world as historical subjects in their own right. Doing so depends on understanding how the archive was made.

## How the archive was described

The finding aids that give access to the VOC archives, from historical tables of contents to modern inventories and indexes, are based on document descriptions. These usually record who wrote a document, when, where and to whom, and what it was about when this was relevant to the people who created and later managed the archive. The most detailed finding aid for the series that Globalise works with, the [TANAP index](https://docs.globalise.huygens.knaw.nl/tanap/tanap-index-establishment-reconstructions/), also assigns each document to a category, and these categories were explicitly created from the perspective of the VOC.

As a result, the most visible people in these descriptions are company servants, and the most visible subjects are trade and production, even when the documents themselves also deal with local individuals, communities and other subjects. Globalise tries to go beyond these descriptions by identifying people, places, events and other entities within the documents themselves. See [Methods](/pages/about/methods.md).

## Colonial terminology and categories

The documents use the language of their time and of their writers. They contain terms and descriptions of people that are offensive, and categories, for instance for ethnic or religious groups, that were imposed by colonial authorities. Some of the archival metadata is also harmful or opaque: it can discriminate against, erase or belittle marginalised people.

These terms and categories are part of the historical record, and they are themselves a subject of research: how groups were labelled and represented is one of the questions the Globalise infrastructure was designed to support. But they should never be taken over uncritically. When you come across such a term, ask who used it, about whom, and for what purpose.

### Place names

The names of places illustrate the problem well. A place can be known by a name given by the VOC or another colonial power, by historical or present-day local names, and by names now widely used in English. Because the project depends largely on colonial sources in Dutch and English, the names used by colonial powers have a higher chance of being recorded than local names, which are often hard to recover without colonial documentation.

The Globalise places dataset tries to address this by including alternative local and colonial names. It chooses a preferred name for each place based on findability, ethical considerations and clarity, with GeoNames as the main source. Experts from India, Indonesia and Taiwan have reviewed and added to the data. See the [ethics report on the places dataset](/pages/ethics/ethics-policy.md#places-dataset) for details.
